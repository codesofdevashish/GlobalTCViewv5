        Be ready

        General guidance. Always follow your national weather service and local authorities.

        When a cyclone may come

        • Follow official bulletins on radio, TV or your weather service's app.
        • Keep drinking water, food, medicines, a torch, a power bank and copies of documents ready for three days.
        • Know your nearest cyclone shelter and the safest route to it.
        • Tie down or bring in loose objects that strong wind can throw.

        When a warning is issued

        • Move to a shelter or strong building early if you live near the sea, in a low area or in a weak house.
        • Fishers: do not go to sea, and return to harbour.
        • Stay indoors, away from windows. A sudden calm can be the eye: the wind will return from the other side.
        • Switch off electricity and gas if water may enter.

        After it passes

        • Wait for the all-clear before going out.
        • Keep away from fallen power lines, floodwater and damaged buildings.
        • Drink only boiled or treated water.
        • Check on elderly neighbours and people living alone.

        What this site is

        A research project that follows every tropical cyclone on Earth, rebuilt from NOAA's GFS weather model and official storm advisories several times a day. The live map shows the winds, pressure, moisture and sea temperature that steer and feed storms. Storm analysis has 3D views of each storm's flow, its environment and an impact estimate. Learn explains how it all works.

        It is not an official warning service. For warnings, use your national meteorological service.

                  How the atmosphere makes and moves tropical cyclones

                  Five short explanations, each linked to what you can see on the live map today.

                  The three circulation cells

                  The tropics receive far more sunlight than the poles. The atmosphere evens this out by turning over in three great loops in each hemisphere.

                  In the Hadley cell, air rises near the equator, where the trade winds meet in a band of towering clouds called the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ). It flows towards the poles high up, sinks around 30° latitude, and returns along the surface as the trade winds. That sinking air makes the subtropical highs and many of the world's deserts. The Ferrel cell between about 30° and 60° is driven by the other two; at the surface it gives the westerlies. The polar cell sinks cold air over the poles.

                  Most tropical cyclones form on the equator side of the subtropical highs, in the moist air of the Hadley cell, and are steered by the winds around those highs.

                  The Walker circulation, El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole

                  Along the equator there is a second, east–west loop. Normally the trade winds push warm water to the western Pacific, where air rises and rain is heavy; it sinks over the cooler eastern Pacific. This is the Walker circulation.

                  During El Niño the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and the rising branch shifts towards the central Pacific. During La Niña the normal pattern is stronger. Scientists track this with the sea temperature in the Niño 3.4 region (5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W): half a degree above or below normal for several months marks El Niño or La Niña.

                  The Indian Ocean has its own see-saw, the Indian Ocean Dipole, measured by the difference in sea temperature between a western and an eastern box. Both patterns shift where and how often cyclones form, including over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.

                  What a tropical cyclone needs

                  • Warm sea water, at least about 26.5 °C. On the live map, colour by sea surface temperature: the dashed white line marks 26.5 °C.
                  • Moist air through a deep layer. Colour the map by water vapour to see it.
                  • Weak vertical wind shear: the wind should not change much between the lower and upper atmosphere, or it tears the storm apart. Compare 850 and 200 hPa on the live map.
                  • A spinning start: an existing disturbance away from the equator, where the Earth's rotation can start the swirl.

                  When these come together, warm moist air rises in thunderstorms, the pressure falls, the winds speed up and draw in more moist air. In the strongest storms an eye forms: a calm, clear centre surrounded by the eyewall, where the winds are fiercest.

                    How strong is it?

                    Different agencies average the wind over different times, so their numbers differ slightly. In the North Indian Ocean, the India Meteorological Department uses these classes (3-minute winds):

                    ClassWindWhat it can do
                    Depression, Deep Depression31–61 km/hHeavy rain, rough sea
                    Cyclonic Storm62–88 km/hDamage to huts and trees, very rough sea
                    Severe Cyclonic Storm89–117 km/hDamage to thatched roofs, power and communication lines
                    Very Severe Cyclonic Storm118–166 km/hExtensive damage to weak houses, roads and crops; storm surge
                    Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm167–221 km/hLarge-scale damage; high storm surge
                    Super Cyclonic Storm222 km/h or moreCatastrophic damage and surge

                    Elsewhere the Saffir–Simpson scale (1-minute winds) is used: category 1 from 119 km/h to category 5 at 252 km/h or more.

                    Reading the live map

                    The moving lines are the wind at the chosen height. Near the surface, winds blow almost along the isobars, anticlockwise around lows in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern. Higher up you can see the jet streams, and the outflow above strong storms.

                    The white lines are isobars (lines of equal pressure at sea level) near the surface, and lines of equal height of the pressure level higher up: low heights are troughs, high heights are ridges. H and L mark highs and lows. Click anywhere on the map to read the values there.

                      To model and estimate the spatial patterns of cyclone vulnerability and resulting damage, we integrate cyclone hazard dynamics, exposure (population and economic wealth) and social vulnerability into a unified spatial framework.

                      1. Data integration

                      All variables are mapped onto a standardised two-dimensional grid of 0.1° cells with coordinates (x, y), cut out around each storm's track.

                      2. Hazard H(x, y)

                      The wind field follows the Holland (1980) profile, V(r) = Vmax · [ (Rmax/r)B · exp(1 − (Rmax/r)B) ]½, driven hour by hour by the official track and intensity (NCEP TCVitals). The shape parameter B = ρ e Vmax2 / Δp (limited to 1–2.5) uses the pressure deficit Δp between the environment and the centre; the radius of maximum wind Rmax comes from TCVitals (Willoughby et al. 2006 when missing). 0.55 × the storm's forward motion is added for the asymmetry. The footprint is the highest wind each cell experiences along the track.

                      3. Exposure E(x, y)

                      Population from GHSL GHS-POP 2025 (EU JRC), summed into each cell. Economic value = population × national GDP per capita (PPP, World Bank), the population-weighted form of LitPop (Eberenz et al. 2020). In the risk index, E = min(1, log10(1 + people) / 4.5).

                      4. Vulnerability V(x, y)

                      A country-level index (0 = least, 1 = most vulnerable): the mean of scaled GDP per capita (PPP), under-5 mortality and lack of access to electricity (World Bank). This first version is deliberately simple and coarse; a gridded deprivation index (GRDI, NASA SEDAC) can replace it.

                      5. Rainfall hazard

                      Rain comes from the GFS: the 0–6 hour accumulated precipitation of each 6-hourly run, so every 6-hour window is covered once. Only rain within 500 km of the storm centre (at the middle of each window) is counted, so that monsoon or frontal rain elsewhere is not attributed to the storm. Two layers are shown: the storm-total accumulation and the largest 24-hour total, in the India Meteorological Department classes (heavy ≥ 64.5 mm, very heavy ≥ 115.6 mm, extremely heavy ≥ 204.5 mm per day). GFS rainfall is a model estimate: it smooths the heaviest bands and can misplace them, so satellite estimates such as GPM IMERG are the natural next step.

                      6. Storm surge (indicative)

                      The surge index (0–100) marks low-lying coast where the sea is most likely to be pushed onshore. It multiplies the square of the strongest modelled wind over the nearby sea (wind stress), a shelf factor that is largest where the sea within about 60 km is shallow, and a height factor for land below about 8 m within 25 km of the sea. Elevation and depth come from NOAA ETOPO1 (1 arc-minute) via NOAA ERDDAP. It is a relative index, not a surge height in metres: real surge also depends on the tide, the angle of approach, the coastline's shape and river mouths. Official surge forecasts come from national services.

                      7. Damage and risk

                      Mean damage ratio f(V) = v3 / (1 + v3), v = max(V − 25.7, 0) / (74.7 − 25.7) with V in m/s (Emanuel 2011, CLIMADA default thresholds). Risk index R(x, y) = 100 · max(Hwind, Hrain) · E · V, with the wind hazard Hwind = min(1, max(0, (Vwind − 17.5 m/s) / (65 − 17.5))), rising from 0 at 34 kt to 1 at about 126 kt, and the rain hazard Hrain = min(1, max(0, (R24 − 64.5 mm) / (300 − 64.5))), rising from 0 at IMD's heavy threshold to 1 at 300 mm in a day. It is shown in five classes from very low (< 1) to very high (> 20) and is relative: no money damage is estimated. The damage ratio f is used for the economic numbers only.

                      Limitations

                      • Wind and rain only. Storm surge, which causes most deaths in the Bay of Bengal, and river flooding are not modelled.
                      • The damage curve is not calibrated for each region, so the index compares places and storms rather than predicting losses.
                      • Vulnerability is national, so differences within a country are not captured.
                      • Population is a 2025 estimate; official intensities have their own uncertainty.

                      For comparison, the Global Disaster Alert and Coordination System (GDACS) publishes exposed-population estimates for every storm.

                      Rapid intensification (RI)
                      An increase of at least 30 kt in the maximum sustained wind within 24 hours. Most of the strongest storms go through it, and it is hard to forecast.
                      Maximum sustained wind
                      The highest wind averaged over 1 minute (US agencies, JTWC), 3 minutes (IMD) or 10 minutes (most other agencies). The same storm therefore has different numbers in different bulletins; 10-minute winds are roughly 0.93 × 1-minute winds.
                      IMD classes
                      India Meteorological Department scale for the North Indian Ocean: Depression 17–27 kt, Deep Depression 28–33, Cyclonic Storm 34–47, Severe 48–63, Very Severe 64–89, Extremely Severe 90–119, Super Cyclonic Storm 120 kt or more (3-minute winds).
                      Saffir–Simpson categories
                      Category 1 is 64–82 kt, 2 is 83–95, 3 is 96–112, 4 is 113–136 and 5 is 137 kt or more (1-minute winds). Categories 3–5 are called major.
                      Invest
                      An area of disturbed weather that an agency is investigating (numbered 90–99). It receives a storm number when it becomes a tropical depression in that agency's analysis.
                      Vertical wind shear
                      The change of the horizontal wind with height, here between 850 and 200 hPa (deep layer) or 850 and 500 hPa. Shear tilts the vortex and brings dry air into the core; under about 10 m/s it rarely stops intensification.
                      Vortex tilt
                      The horizontal distance between the circulation centres at 850 and 500 hPa. A vertically aligned vortex (small tilt) is a common precursor of rapid intensification.
                      Radius of maximum wind (RMW)
                      The distance from the centre to the strongest winds. It usually contracts as a storm intensifies.
                      Accumulated cyclone energy (ACE)
                      The sum of the squared maximum winds (kt) every 6 hours while a storm is at least tropical-storm strength, divided by 10,000. It combines how strong, how long-lived and how many storms there were.
                      Ensemble forecast
                      The same model run many times from slightly different starting states. The spread of the tracks shows how uncertain the forecast is; the ensemble mean is often more accurate than a single run.
                      ENSO, IOD, MJO
                      El Niño–Southern Oscillation (Pacific), Indian Ocean Dipole and Madden–Julian Oscillation: large-scale patterns that shift where and how often tropical cyclones form.
                      Storm surge
                      The rise of sea level pushed onshore by the storm's winds and low pressure. In the Bay of Bengal it causes most cyclone deaths.
